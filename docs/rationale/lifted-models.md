# Lifted models: what discovering the equations adds

Status: draft, 2026-10-24. The next topic after `convergence.md`. It describes what becomes
possible once a system dynamics model can be **lifted** from raw measurements by an algorithm
instead of written by an expert. It also says what must be proven before any of it is relied on.

Grounding: SD Lab (`~/orchestra/sdlab`), its discovery engine, the ladder, the demos, and
`OPERATIONALIZATION.md`. Numbers below are quoted from those documents as of 2026-10-24.

---

## 1. What is new

For sixty years, system dynamics models have been **built by hand**. Experts interview the people
who run a system, draw its loops, write its equations, and calibrate some numbers against data.
That's slow, expensive, and dependent on judgement. The models go stale, and organisations that
use SD keep staff to maintain them. Validation is mostly argument: does the structure match what
people know, and does the behaviour look right? (Forrester and Senge 1980; Barlas 1996.)

A **lifted** model is found from measured levels alone, by a fixed algorithm at a fixed commit. It
names:
- the flows;
- their rate equations;
- the stocks nobody measured;
- the delays;
- the caps.

Given the same data and code, it gives the same model, so anyone can rerun it and get the same
answer.

That adds verbs SD never had cheaply:

| Verb | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lift** | Find a model from data. |
| **Re-lift** | Find it again after time passes or after a change. |
| **Diff** | Compare two lifted models: what changed in the structure or the numbers. |
| **Predict a structure** | Say, before a change, what the next lifted model should look like. |
| **Test a structure** | Check the prediction against the re-lifted model. |

Those five verbs are what this document is about.

## 2. Where discovery stands (honest status)

From SD Lab's own record. These numbers bound every claim below.

**Simulated SD systems: works on small systems, short of its own goal**
- On the ladder of small true models, the leading strategy passes most cells: combined2 has 852
  passes plus 161 equivalent out of about 1,150. Noisy cells are harder: 124 of 156.
- The kubernetes rungs 300–316 (up to 14 stocks and 23 flows, including a hidden memory leak and
  an OOM table) pass on the variants judged achievable. 317 (36 stocks, 62 flows) does not pass.
- The realistic examples' goal (skill@20 ≥ 90%, skill@100 ≥ 80% on every example) has never been
  met. The best strategies meet it on about 15–21 of about 62–66 runs, with mean skill@20 around
  30–50%.
- Exact equation recovery on realistic examples is near zero.
- Large models can predict well without having recovered the structure. On sd-k8s-platform
  (1,130 flows), blockwise reaches 99.4% skill but only about 205 of 794 parts right. **Good
  prediction is not the same as the right structure.**

**Data not generated by SD models: unproven**
- On 52 published real-data cases, no strategy agrees with any published structure, and held-out
  skill is about zero.
- On particle (discrete agent) systems, every strategy scores 59–105 points below the mean-field
  model.
- In demo 16 (a discrete-event on-call simulation), the lifted model's structure "is not the
  system's". It confirmed a good agent and underrated a weak one. **It gave a wrong "yes" to an
  agent that closed items wrongly, because wrong closes look like resolutions in the counts.**
- Discovery on real Prometheus data has not been shown to work.

**Confidence without the truth: partial**
- The evidence score reaches AUC 0.87 within its training sample, but 0.66 on truly held-out
  variants. Its strongest features measure agreement between strategies, not correctness.
- Some structures can't be told apart from the data at all. With both stocks measured, a move
  can't be distinguished from a link plus a drain.
- Diffs between two lifted models are only as good as both lifts. One quiet-day diff produced 12
  false edits.

The claim to make to colleagues is therefore **not** "we can discover the true equations of any
system". It is: **"we can lift a reproducible, testable model, state how confident we are, and
check its predictions. Section 8 is the programme for earning trust on real data."**

## 3. Predictions as acceptance tests

Software has unit tests for code and acceptance tests for features. Lifted models make a third
kind possible: **tests on a system's behaviour**, stated as the structure we expect the lifted
model to have after a change.

### 3.1 The intended-effect test

Before a task starts, it states its effect as a model edit:
- "The OOM-kill flow's dependence on memory pressure falls by half."
- "The rework fraction out of review drops from 0.20 to below 0.12."
- "A new balancing loop appears: queue depth throttles intake."

After the change has run long enough (the horizon is part of the test), the system is re-lifted
and the edit checked. The outcomes differ in kind:

| Outcome | Meaning |
|---|---|
| The predicted edit appears, and the leading stocks move as simulated | Passed. |
| The predicted edit appears, but the stocks don't move as simulated | Our model of the rest of the system is wrong. Discovery goes there. |
| No edit appears | The change didn't do what we think. The work was wrong or never took effect. |
| A different edit appears | A side effect. It's now visible and named. |

A ticket's acceptance criterion can include an intended-effect test. Its status is then decided by
the system, not by the agent that did the work. Reward hacking (editing tests until they pass)
doesn't work against it, **provided the sensors are outside every agent's write access**.

### 3.2 The null test

Much work should *not* change behaviour: refactors, library upgrades, migrations, infrastructure
moves, a model version swap behind a stable interface. For these the test is **"the re-lifted
model is the same as before, within tolerance"**.

That's a regression test on behaviour, the kind no code-level test suite provides. Its value
depends entirely on the **false-change rate**: how often a quiet period produces a diff anyway.
That has to be measured per system before null tests are trusted, and the 12-false-edit quiet day
above says it isn't small yet.

### 3.3 The unexplained-change test

A re-lifted model that differs from the last, with **no declared change** to explain it, is a
finding:
- another team deployed something;
- traffic changed character;
- a dependency degraded;
- an agent's behaviour drifted (memory, model version, prompt).

Joining diffs with the change log (deploys, flags, config, agent configuration) turns each
unexplained edit into a lead, as Azure's Gandalf does for rollouts.

### 3.4 Tests at different horizons

Lifted-model tests are slow compared with unit tests, because they need data. So each layer of
work has a test at its own speed:

| Layer | Test | Speed |
|---|---|---|
| Code | Unit and acceptance tests | Seconds to minutes |
| Behaviour (dense flows) | Re-lift on dense stocks: test runs, queue lengths, OOM kills | Hours to days |
| Outcome (sparse stocks) | Simulation from the re-lifted model, then data as it accrues | Weeks |

## 4. Tasks stated as dynamics

A task can be written as a change in behaviour instead of in prose: "improve the merge pipeline's
success rate by 30%". With a lifted model, that sentence carries a lot of information.

1. **The target is located.** The model has a success flow out of the gate and the flows that
   compete with it: failures by cause, flaky reruns, conflicts, timeouts. Each has an equation.
2. **The levers are ranked.** Sensitivity analysis on the model (SD Lab's leverage, extended to
   parameters and structures) lists what could produce +30%. For example:
   - halve the flake-induced failure fraction;
   - shrink the batch size, which lowers the bisection rate;
   - rebase before landing, which cuts conflicts;
   - with their predicted sizes and their side effects (throughput, CI load).
3. **The task becomes an inverse problem.** Find the cheapest change to controllable parameters
   or structures whose simulated effect is +30% with nothing else made worse. Sometimes the model
   alone answers it, e.g. "batch size 6 → 3 gives +27% at 18% more CI time". Then the work left is
   mostly implementation.
4. **The acceptance test writes itself:** the intended-effect test of section 3.1, with the
   simulated edit.

The agent doing the work receives a **concrete target with a mechanism**, not a slogan. The
vaguer the task in prose, the more the model supplies. How much it can supply depends on how much
of the relevant structure has been lifted, and how confidently.

## 5. Models as context for agents

An agent asked to change a system usually gets prose, code, and logs. A lifted model gives it
something better:

- **The relevant subgraph:** the stocks and flows near the target, their equations, and the loops
  through them.
- **The levers:** sensitivities, and which loop dominates in the current regime.
- **A simulator as a tool:** the agent can try "what if I change X" before touching the system,
  and include the simulated effect in its plan.
- **Its own acceptance test:** the intended-effect test.
- **History:** past eras, what was tried, and what the re-lift showed.

This is **simulate-before-act**. It also gives the agent a vocabulary for its report: "I expect
the rework fraction to fall; here's the simulation", which a reviewer, human or agent, can check
against the re-lift later.

Simulations are cheap (thousands per second) next to agent turns. Moving exploration from the live
system into the model is also a cost argument.

## 6. Designed where we know, lifted where we don't

The classic objection to models of human systems is the Lucas critique: change the policy and the
behaviour you estimated changes too.

Agent orchestration has an unusual answer. **The decision rules of the machines we deploy are
known**, because we wrote them: the agents' prompts and tools, the programs' code, the loop's
policies. Their equations don't have to be discovered. They are declared, and they change exactly
when we change them, as announced eras.

What has to be lifted is the rest:
- the environment (people, production, other teams);
- how our machines *actually* behave against what they were told: compliance, quality, variance.

SD Lab already supports this split. `opts.known` subtracts declared flows so discovery explains
only what the model is missing. The split:
- **shrinks the search,** which matters because run time grows steeply with the number of flows;
- **improves identifiability,** since fewer unknowns compete for the same data;
- **turns policy changes into clean experiments:** the declared part changes by design, and the
  lifted part shows whether the world responded as the declared model predicted.

A machine whose lifted behaviour drifts from its declared rule is itself a finding: an agent
ignoring its brief, or a program with a bug.

## 7. The universal machine

### 7.1 What SD was always about

Forrester's purpose for system dynamics was **policy design**. A policy is the rule that sets a
flow's rate from the information available at the point of decision. SD's lesson was that
behaviour comes from structure and policy, so you improve a system by redesigning its decision
rules, not by exhorting the people in it.

The hard part was always **implementation**. A redesigned policy had to be carried out by people.
People follow rules imperfectly, misperceive delays and accumulation (Sterman's beer game), drift
back under pressure, and can't be reassigned to a new flow overnight. The gap between the policy
on paper and the policy in practice was where SD projects died.

### 7.2 What an agent is, in those terms

An AI agent is a **machine that can be placed on any flow** and given any decision rule that can
be written down:
- the rule is written in its brief and tools;
- it can be moved to another flow in minutes;
- its capacity scales with money, not hiring;
- every action it takes is logged.

The nearest historical precedents:
- Forrester's "management laboratory" for pretesting policies (1961).
- Stafford Beer's adaptive "cybernetic factory" (c. 1960). He belonged to the cybernetic tradition
  rather than SD's, though Project Cybersyn ran an SD simulator.
- Wiener's 1949 warning to the UAW about general-purpose control machines inside industrial
  feedback loops.

None of them, as far as the research found, wrote about a machine that could fill *any* role.
The claim here is ours: SD treats every role as a policy, so a machine that can carry out any
written policy makes the policy itself the designed artefact. That's what SD was built to
analyse. They had to work through people. We don't.

So policy design becomes **policy deployment**:
1. Find leverage in the model.
2. Write the decision rule.
3. Place an agent (or a program) on the flow.
4. Re-lift to see whether the world responded.

### 7.3 What that makes possible

- **Reallocating machines by leverage.** Capacity moves to the flows where simulation says it
  pays, and away when the bottleneck moves. The factory reconfigures itself.
- **Policies tested in simulation first.** Including the policies that govern the agents
  themselves: how many to start, when to stop, how strict the critic is.
- **The supply line counted.** An orchestrator that starts work without counting what's already
  in review and in CI floods the pipeline and then oscillates. That's the beer game's central
  finding. A machine can carry out the corrective rule (start work against desired pipeline minus
  current pipeline) exactly, where people characteristically don't.
- **Agents in SD's other jobs.** Drafting structure (LLMs already draft stock-and-flow models with
  some skill: SDBot, LunaSim), running experiments, reading diffs, writing change notes. That
  automates the "large staff" that maintaining SD models used to need.

### 7.4 What the universal machine is not

- **It isn't perfectly compliant.** Agents deviate from their brief (MAST's "disobey task
  specification": 11.8% of failures), game their tests (METR), and drift with memory. That
  deviation is measurable by section 6's split, and it's a stock-like property the model tracks.
- **It isn't free.** Token use varies up to 30× for the same task. Cost is a flow from a budget
  stock, and it belongs in the model.
- **It doesn't remove people.** Human review, approval, and judgement stay as flows with their own
  capacity and fatigue. Often they are the binding constraint.

## 8. The validation programme: earning trust on real data

None of sections 3–7 should be relied on until discovery is shown to work on data that SD models
didn't generate. The programme, in order of increasing realism. Each stage uses its own data and
gates the uses that need it.

**V1. Discrete-event simulations of agent work**
- Extend demo 16. Build simulations of an agent orchestrator: discrete tickets, integer
  reviewers, heavy-tailed service, rework with delay, critic leniency, review fatigue, budget
  limits.
- Many seeds; announced policy changes.
- Score:
  - held-out forecast skill against persistence;
  - **intervention prediction:** the predicted against the actual effect of a policy change
    unseen in training, against a queueing-network baseline and a forecasting baseline;
  - **verdict accuracy:** did the lifted model reach the right yes, partly, or no on agent
    variants, including a misrouting agent?
  - **null-test false-change rate** on quiet periods;
  - **detection power** for a known structural change of a given size.
- Nardini et al. (2021) is the precedent: equations learned from agent-based simulations, which
  predicted better than the mean-field model where it failed.

**V2. Agent-based and particle systems.** Heterogeneous actors and network effects. These are
where aggregate models are known to struggle (Rahmandad and Sterman 2008), so they say where the
limit lies.

**V3. Orchestra's own history**
- Real data with no truth.
- Score held-out forecasts and announced-era predictions.
- Check effect estimates with CausalImpact.
- Measure the false-change rate on periods with no declared change.

**V4. One production service from Prometheus,** with its owners' agreement. A designed skeleton
from the owners plus lifted numbers, compared against a fully lifted model.

**V5. The work system of one person's job.** Sparse and discrete. The density rules in
`../proposals/system-dynamics-core.md` (section 3) apply.

**What each use needs before it's relied on:**

| Use | Needs |
|---|---|
| Orientation (maps, context for agents) | V1 forecast skill clearly above persistence |
| Intended-effect tests | V1 verdict accuracy, with no wrong "yes" on the misrouting class |
| Null tests | A measured false-change rate per system, low enough to act on |
| Tasks as inverse problems | V1 intervention prediction beating the queueing baseline |
| Credit for prevention | V3/V4 forecasts with intervals that hold |

**What demo 16 already teaches:** if a flow's failures look like successes in the counts, no
amount of discovery separates them. Wrong closes looked like resolutions. **Rework must be
measured directly before an agent ships.** That's a rule about choosing stocks (sensed outcomes,
not reported ones), not about the algorithm.

## 9. Risks particular to lifted models

- **Good fit isn't truth.** Large models predict well with the wrong structure (section 2).
  Structural claims need structural evidence: invariance across eras, discriminating experiments,
  and proofs of equivalence where SD Lab has them.
- **The model becomes a target.** If agents are judged by the re-lifted model, they will be
  tempted to move the sensors rather than the system. Sensors must sit outside every agent's write
  access. The paired-opposite rule (every goal stock with its counter-stock) applies.
- **Reflexivity.** Once people and agents act on the model's predictions, the system changes
  because of the model. Eras mark those changes; nothing else makes them interpretable.
- **Method bias.** Two strategies can lift different, equally fitting models. Report the
  disagreement; don't pick silently.
- **Identifiability.** When the data can't tell two structures apart, the right response is an
  experiment that would, e.g. a flag toggle or a load step in staging. Agents can run those, under
  the same approval rules as any change.
- **Cost of re-lifting.** Run time grows steeply with flows. Warm starts from the last model,
  re-lifting only the regions that changed, and small coupled models instead of one big one are
  prerequisites, not optimisations.
